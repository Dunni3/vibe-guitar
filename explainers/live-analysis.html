Guitar FX box · design notes

What the Live page is looking at

The web app's Live page shows what the box hears and plays, about 30 times a second: levels, a spectrum, the note you're playing, a chord guess, the beat, the noise floor, and how the output compares with the input. It can also record. This page explains each panel, the math behind it, and why none of it can make the audio click.

Audio side
callback → tap ring

The engine's audio callback copies every input and output sample into a shared-memory ring. That copy is a few hundred bytes per block, takes no locks, and never waits for anyone.

Analysis side
ring → history → phone

The web server reads the ring at low priority, keeps 90 s of history, and computes analysis frames only while someone has the Live page open.

01 · The data path

A ring the audio thread writes and never waits on

The audio callback is the function the audio server (JACK) calls once per buffer, with a hard deadline. Anything that might block it (a lock, a memory allocation, a disk write) risks a click: an audible gap where a buffer wasn't ready in time. So the analysis can't be done there, and the callback can't hand data over in any way that waits.

The handover is the tap: a file in /dev/shm/fxbox-tap (shared memory, so it lives in RAM and never touches the SD card) holding a ring of 218 frames, about 4 s at 48 kHz. A frame is one sample per channel (for example "in" and "out"). The C++ engine writes each block into the ring and then publishes a counter, write_pos, the total frames ever written. The data lives at pos % capacity, so the newest block overwrites the oldest.

  • The writer (audio thread) is wait-free: a bounded copy and one atomic store, with no allocation and no system calls. It doesn't know whether anyone is reading.
  • The reader (a thread in the web server, which runs niced, that is, at lower scheduling priority) keeps its own position. Each pass it copies everything between its position and write_pos into a 90 s in-memory history.
  • If the reader falls more than a full ring behind, the frames it missed are gone. It jumps forward to near the oldest frame still in the ring (keeping 1/8 of a ring as a safety margin) and adds the gap to a dropped counter. After copying it re-reads write_pos and throws away anything the writer lapped mid-copy, counting that too. The analysis has a hole; the audio is untouched.

On top of the history, an analysis thread builds one JSON frame about every 33 ms and pushes it to the phone over Server-Sent Events (SSE: one long HTTP response the server keeps appending lines to). With nobody watching, it does nothing.

The ring is drawn as 32 slots, each 1/32 of the real 4 s ring (8192 frames, about 170 ms), and time runs about twice as fast as real. The amber head writes at the audio rate no matter what. The teal reader normally drains in bursts and stays close. Tick "slow reader" and it gets so far behind that the writer laps it: those blocks are overwritten before they were read and count as dropped. The writer never slows down.

02 · Levels, noise floor, hum

How loud, how close to the ceiling, how quiet underneath

Samples are floats between −1 and +1. Full scale (±1) is the most the converter can represent; anything beyond is flattened, which is clipping and sounds like harsh crackle. Levels are shown in dBFS, decibels relative to full scale:

level_dBFS = 20 · log10(|x|) 0 dBFS = full scale −6 dBFS ≈ half full scale −20 dBFS = one tenth

Each analysis frame looks at the samples that arrived since the previous frame (about 1600 per channel) and reports two numbers per channel:

  • Peak: the largest |x|. This is what clips. A frame counts as clipped when any |x| ≥ 0.999.
  • RMS (root mean square, √mean(x²)): the average power, closer to how loud it sounds. A pure sine's RMS sits 3 dB below its peak; a plucked string sits much further below, because each pluck starts with a sharp spike. That gap is the crest factor, which the page also shows.

Set the Pisound's gain knob so that your hardest strums peak between −12 and −6 dBFS. Higher leaves no room for a surprise; much lower wastes the converter's resolution and lifts the relative noise.

noise floor

The 5th percentile of frame RMS over 60 s

Every frame's input RMS goes into a one-minute history. The quietest 5% of that history is the noise floor: what's left when you aren't playing (preamp hiss, pickup noise, the room). A percentile rather than the minimum, so one freak silent frame doesn't set it. Below −52 dBFS the guitar counts as "quiet" and pitch, chords and onsets are switched off.

hum

60, 120 and 180 Hz, measured only while quiet

Mains electricity alternates at 60 Hz (in North America), and bad grounding leaks it into audio at 60 Hz and its multiples. The open low E string's partials sit near 82, 165 Hz, close enough to confuse things, so hum is measured only when the RMS is within 6 dB of the noise floor. It's flagged when it stands 12 dB above the surrounding 40–400 Hz spectrum.

brightness

Spectral centroid

The magnitude-weighted mean frequency above 50 Hz, Σ f·|X(f)| ÷ Σ |X(f)|: the "centre of mass" of the spectrum. Picking near the bridge or with a thin pick moves it up; picking near the neck or with the thumb moves it down.

03 · Spectrum and spectrogram

Which frequencies are present, at a price in time

A spectrum splits the last stretch of signal into how much energy sits at each frequency. Every frame takes the latest N = 8192 samples of each channel, multiplies them by a Hann window (a smooth bump that fades the ends to zero, so the cut edges don't splatter fake energy across all frequencies), and runs an FFT (fast Fourier transform):

X[k] = Σₙ w[n] · x[n] · e^(−2πi·k·n/N) k = 0 … N/2 bin spacing Δf = fs / N = 48000 / 8192 ≈ 5.9 Hz window T = N / fs = 8192 / 48000 ≈ 171 ms Δf · T = 1

The last line is the catch. The resolution in frequency is the reciprocal of the stretch of time you look at. You can't have 1 Hz bins and 10 ms timing from one FFT; it's the same uncertainty relation as a Gaussian's width versus its Fourier transform's width. A note on the low E string (82.4 Hz) and the F one fret up (87.3 Hz) are 4.9 Hz apart. With a Hann window, two equal tones show as separate peaks only when they're about two bins apart or more, so 8192 points (0.84 bins apart) blur them into one lump; it takes 32768 points, a 0.68 s window, to show two peaks. That's why the tuner doesn't read pitch off the spectrum (section 04), and why the chord guess (section 05) leans on the higher partials, where a semitone is many bins wide.

The magnitudes are scaled by 2 / Σw, so a full-scale sine reads 0 dBFS, then mapped onto 240 log-spaced display bins from 30 Hz to 16 kHz (each about 2.7% wider than the last, so every octave gets the same width on screen, as your ear hears it). At the top, one display bin covers many FFT bins and takes their maximum; at the bottom, display bins are narrower than FFT bins and are interpolated. The spectrogram stacks those spectra over the last 20 s, time across and frequency up, with brightness for level: a pluck is a bright vertical streak that fades upward first, because high partials die faster.

The click's energy is spread over every frequency, so it lifts the floor everywhere, more at small sizes where it's a bigger share of the window.

04 · Tuner: pitch by YIN

Find the period, not the loudest peak

The pitch of a note is set by its fundamental frequency f₀, the rate at which the whole waveform repeats. A string also vibrates at 2f₀, 3f₀, 4f₀… (its partials or harmonics), and on a piezo pickup the fundamental is often not the loudest of them. So "biggest FFT peak" gives octave errors, and the FFT's 5.9 Hz bins are about 120 cents wide at the low E (a cent is 1/100 of a semitone, a frequency ratio of 21/1200). Instead the tuner asks: what shift τ makes the signal line up best with itself? That's YIN (de Cheveigné and Kawahara, 2002), run on the input channel after averaging sample pairs down to 24 kHz, with a window of W = 1024 samples (43 ms) and τ searched over 17…401 samples (1400 Hz down to 60 Hz).

1. difference d(τ) = Σ_{j<W} (x[j] − x[j+τ])² 2. normalise d'(τ) = d(τ) · τ / Σ_{k=1..τ} d(k), d'(0) = 1 3. threshold first τ with d'(τ) < 0.15, then slide down to its local minimum 4. refine fit a parabola through d(τ−1), d(τ), d(τ+1) → τ* = τ + shift f₀ = 24000 / τ*, confidence = 1 − d'(τ)

Step 1 is zero at τ = 0 and dips at every multiple of the period. Expanding the square, d(τ) = e₀ + e_τ − 2r(τ) with r the autocorrelation, so it's computed with FFTs. Step 2 divides by the running mean of d, which removes the trivial dip at τ = 0 and puts every dip on a 0…1 scale. Step 3 takes the first good dip, not the deepest: dips at 2τ, 3τ are often just as deep and would read an octave or a twelfth low. Step 4 matters for accuracy: at 82 Hz the period is about 291 samples, and one whole sample is 6 cents. Interpolating between samples brings it to about a cent.

Then two smoothing rules: the reported note is the median of recent estimates, and a jump of more than 0.7 semitone clears that history, so a new note shows at once instead of gliding.

When the tuner refuses. A chord has a period too: C, E and G share a common period about two octaves below C, a note nobody is playing. So a YIN result is dropped when its pitch class has little energy in the chroma (below 0.4 of the strongest, section 05), and when the chord matcher explains the chroma better than "this one note with its harmonics" does. Harmonics are only shown for a confident estimate (confidence above 0.8).
05 · Harmonics, chroma, chord guess

Folding the spectrum into twelve notes

Harmonics panel. Once the tuner has a confident f₀, the page reads the spectrum near f₀, 2f₀ … 10f₀ (the peak within ±3% or ±2 bins of each) and shows each partial in dB relative to the first. This is the "tone colour" of the note. Don't be surprised when partials 2 or 3 sit above 0 dB: a piezo pickup under the saddle senses the string's force on the bridge, not the air, and it often reports overtones louder than the fundamental. That's a big part of why an under-saddle piezo sounds "quacky" next to a mic, and what body-IR correction (section 07) is meant to fix.

Chroma. A pitch class is a note name regardless of octave: every A (55, 110, 220, 440 Hz…) is pitch class A. The chroma panel takes the spectral peaks between 60 Hz and 2 kHz (local maxima within 40 dB of the loudest, so leakage and noise stay out), converts each peak's frequency to the nearest note, and adds √magnitude into one of 12 bins:

pc(f) = round(69 + 12 · log2(f / 440)) mod 12 0 = C, 9 = A chroma[pc] += √|X(f)| then scale so the largest bin is 1, and smooth: c ← 0.7·c + 0.3·c_new

The chord guess. The chroma vector is compared with templates for 12 roots × 8 chord types (major, minor, 5, 7, maj7, m7, sus2, sus4) by cosine similarity, with small penalties so a 7th chord has to clearly beat the plain triad. Each template includes every chord tone's own harmonics (partials 1–6, weighted 0.6k−1), because real strings bring them.

Here's the trap. Partial 3 is 3f₀, an octave and a fifth up (19 semitones); partial 5 is two octaves and a major third up (27.9 semitones, which rounds to 28). So a single A already puts energy on A, E and C♯: an A major triad. That's why the code doesn't stop at the best chord. It also scores "one note with its harmonics" for the tuner's pitch class, and if that explains the chroma at least as well (within 0.02), it reports a note, not a chord. Treat the chord name as a guess: open strings ringing, a capo or an unusual voicing all move it.

06 · Onsets and tempo

Spotting each pluck, then the beat

An onset is the start of a note: a sudden rise of energy, mostly in the upper partials. The detector uses spectral flux, which asks how much new energy appeared since a moment ago. Every hop of 512 samples (10.7 ms) it takes a 1024-sample Hann-windowed FFT, compresses the magnitudes, and sums only the increases:

S_t[k] = log(1 + 100 · |X_t[k]|) flux_t = Σ_k max(0, S_t[k] − S_{t−1}[k]) (0 while quiet) onset if flux is a local maximum and flux > 1.5 · median(recent) + 0.1 · mean(recent) + 4 and ≥ 60 ms since the last onset

The log compression lets a soft pluck register next to a loud one. Counting only increases ignores the decay of a ringing note. The threshold adapts, following the median of the recent flux, so strumming hard doesn't make every hop an onset and fingerpicking softly isn't missed.

Tempo (in BPM, beats per minute) comes from the flux envelope over the last 8 s. Its autocorrelation, the envelope's correlation with a delayed copy of itself, peaks at lags where the pattern repeats: one beat, two beats, half a beat. Lags are converted to BPM (60 · 93.75 / lag, since there are 93.75 hops per second), restricted to 50–220 BPM, and weighted by a log-normal bump centred on 110 BPM (σ = 0.9 octave). Without that bias, 60 and 120 BPM score almost the same on a steady strum, and the readout flips between them. It's re-estimated once a second, needs at least 8 onsets, and reports nothing if the best peak is weak (under 0.2 of the zero-lag value). A parabola through the peak gives sub-hop precision.

07 · Response panel (2+ channels)

What the effect, or the guitar body, does to each frequency

With two channels, say "in" (x) and "out" (y), the page estimates the transfer function H(f): the gain (and phase) that turns x into y at each frequency. For a linear effect like an EQ, y = h * x (convolution with the effect's impulse response h), so Y(f) = H(f) · X(f). Dividing one spectrum by the other frame by frame is noisy, so it uses the H1 estimator, built from averaged spectra:

Sxx = ⟨|X|²⟩ Syy = ⟨|Y|²⟩ Sxy = ⟨X̄ · Y⟩ ⟨·⟩ = average over frames H1(f) = Sxy / Sxx γ²(f) = |Sxy|² / (Sxx · Syy) 0 ≤ γ² ≤ 1 (coherence)

This is least squares: H1 is the per-frequency coefficient that best predicts Y from X, and γ² is the fraction of the output's power that a linear map of the input explains, the per-frequency R² of that regression. Each frame (when the guitar isn't quiet) takes the last 4096 samples of both channels, windows them, and folds |X|², |Y|² and X̄Y into exponential averages with factor 0.93 (memory of about 14 frames, half a second). The panel redraws every 4th frame on 96 log-spaced bins, and greys out frequencies where the input has no energy, since there's nothing to divide by.

Coherence is the honesty meter. One frame always gives γ² = 1 (any single pair of numbers is "explained" by their ratio), which is why averaging is essential. Over many frames it drops below 1 whenever y contains something that isn't a fixed linear function of the x in the same window:

  • Noise added on the output side (hiss, the room, the other channel's mic picking up the strings directly) is uncorrelated with x.
  • Echoes and reverb: a copy delayed by D contains input from D seconds before the window began. That part has no partner in this frame's X, so it behaves like noise. Reverb is thousands of echoes with a long tail, so it pulls coherence down a lot. A longer FFT helps, at the usual cost in time.
  • Distortion creates frequencies the input didn't have; time-varying effects (chorus, a compressor pumping) change H while it's being averaged.

The delay between the channels comes from GCC-PHAT (generalized cross-correlation with phase transform): normalise Sxy to unit magnitude, keeping only its phase, and inverse-FFT it. A pure delay D has phase e^(−2πifD), whose inverse transform is a spike at lag D. Discarding magnitude makes the spike sharp no matter how coloured the signal is. The peak is searched within ±20 ms and refined with a parabola to a fraction of a sample.

The tone filter is a one-pole lowpass at about 1 kHz: H1 traces it and γ² stays near 1, because it's linear and has a short memory. The echo adds a copy 5 ms (240 samples) later: |H| becomes a comb with notches every 200 Hz, and γ² drops even with no noise at all, because about a quarter of each 21 ms window's echo comes from before the window. Why this panel exists: body-IR correction. The plan for M4 is to make the piezo sound like a mic in front of the guitar. Record the pickup on one input and a small-diaphragm condenser mic on the other, both on one interface so they share one sample clock (two separate devices would drift apart by a few samples a minute and smear the average). Then x = pickup, y = mic, and H1 is exactly the filter to fit: the correction that turns piezo into mic. Coherence says where to trust it. Room echoes and the mic hearing the strings directly lower γ², so the fit should weight each frequency by γ², ignore the low-coherence bins, and keep the mic close to cut room reflections. The fitted filter (an impulse response for convolution) is computed on the laptop, and only the cheap convolution runs on the Pi.
08 · Recording

Takes that survive a pulled plug, and "save what I just played"

Recordings come from the same history the analysis reads, so recording also costs the audio thread nothing. Each take is two files in recordings/, named by date and time:

<id>.wav

24-bit PCM, every channel

WAV is a short header followed by raw samples. PCM means each sample is stored as a plain integer, here 24 bits (about 144 dB of range, far beyond the converters' own noise floor). All tap channels are interleaved in one file, so "in" and "out" stay sample-aligned, which is what the response panel and an IR fit need. Stereo costs 48000 × 2 × 3 bytes, about 17 MB a minute. Single channels can be exported on request.

<id>.json

The sidecar

Name and notes, the preset that was loaded, per-channel peak and clip counts, markers (times you tapped "mark" while playing), the number of frames dropped during the take, and a coarse peak overview (one column per 50 ms) so the list can draw a waveform without opening the WAV.

crash safety

The header is patched every second

A WAV header states the data length, which isn't known until the end. A naive writer fills it in on close, so a power cut leaves a file every player rejects. Here a separate writer thread drains a queue (so a slow SD card can't stall anything upstream) and rewrites the two size fields every second. A cut loses at most the last second. On startup, takes still marked "recording" are recovered from whatever reached the disk. A take also stops itself before the SD card drops below 300 MB free.

retroactive

"Save last N s"

The server always keeps the last 90 s in memory. The button copies the last N seconds of that history (up to what's there) straight to a new take: the good version you played before you thought to press record.
