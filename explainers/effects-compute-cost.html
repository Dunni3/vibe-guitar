What guitar effects cost to compute

A digital effect runs a small piece of arithmetic on every sample of audio. At 48 kHz that happens 48,000 times a second, so the useful way to measure an effect is operations per sample: how many multiplies and adds it needs before the next sample arrives.

All counts here are per sample, for one channel (your acoustic is mono), in 32-bit float. They're planning estimates that tell you roughly where each effect sits. Real code can land within about 2× either way.

The whole picture on one scale

The scale is logarithmic, so every gridline is ten times the last. The dashed lines show how many CPU cycles each sample gets on the two boxes you're weighing, if you count one operation as roughly one cycle.

Budget at 48 kHz: Daisy Seed, 480 MHz ÷ 48,000 = 10,000 cycles per sample. One Pi 5 core, 2.4 GHz ÷ 48,000 = 50,000 cycles per sample, and the Pi has four cores.

Almost everything sits two to three orders of magnitude below the budget. The one thing that gets close is convolution done the naive way, which is why the FFT trick in the last section matters.

What counts as one operation

One operation is a single floating-point multiply or add. Reading a value from memory, comparing two numbers, and looking something up in a table are roughly as cheap, so they get counted too. Expensive math functions such as log, exp and sin cost 10–30 operations each when written as fast approximations, and that's why effects that need them per sample sit higher up.

Both your candidate chips have a fused multiply-add instruction that does a + b × c in one step. Most audio code is chains of exactly that, so in practice a multiply-and-add pair often costs one cycle, not two.

Tier 1 · around 10 operations

One EQ band or filter

First, what these controls are. Any sound is a mix of frequencies, measured in hertz (Hz, vibrations per second). Low frequencies are the bass, like the boom of the guitar body; your low E string's fundamental is about 82 Hz. High frequencies are the treble, like pick attack and string zing, up into the thousands of hertz (kHz). A filter makes some frequencies louder or quieter and leaves the rest alone.

  • Tone control: the bass and treble knobs on an amp or stereo. Turning treble down makes the high frequencies quieter.
  • EQ (equalizer): a set of more precise tone controls. Each band is one control aimed at one range, such as "cut a little around 1.2 kHz" or "boost a little around 100 Hz".
  • Low-cut, also called a high-pass filter: removes everything below a frequency you pick. On an acoustic it gets rid of boomy rumble and feedback below about 80 Hz.
  • High-cut, also called a low-pass filter: removes everything above a frequency, for example harsh fizz above 8 kHz.
  • Peaking band: a bump or dip around one frequency. Q sets how narrow it is: a high Q touches a thin slice, a low Q a broad region. A very narrow dip is called a notch.
  • Shelf: raises or lowers everything above a point (high shelf) or below it (low shelf) by the same amount. A treble knob is a high shelf.

Changes in loudness are measured in decibels (dB). 0 dB means no change, −6 dB is roughly half the amplitude, and +6 dB is roughly double. In the plot further down, the vertical axis is dB and the horizontal axis is frequency.

Under the hood, all of these are usually the same tiny filter, called a biquad. It remembers the last two input samples and the last two output samples, and mixes them with five fixed numbers called coefficients. Which kind of filter you get depends only on those five numbers.

y[n] = b0·x[n] + b1·x[n−1] + b2·x[n−2] − a1·y[n−1] − a2·y[n−2]

Five multiplies and four adds give nine operations, plus shuffling the four remembered values along: that's the "around 10". Turning an EQ knob only recomputes the five coefficients. The per-sample cost stays the same whether the band is a gentle shelf or a narrow notch. A 10-band graphic EQ is ten of these in a row, about 100 operations.

Tier 2 · tens to a few hundred operations

Compressor, chorus, phaser, delay

These effects combine a few cheap building blocks: filters, a delay line (a ring buffer of past samples), a slow LFO (a low-frequency oscillator that wobbles a parameter), and some gain math. Each block costs a handful of operations, so totals land in the tens. Stereo versions, extra voices or oversampling push them toward a few hundred.

Compressor

A compressor watches the signal's level in a side path and turns the gain down when it gets loud. The expensive step is that "how loud" is judged in decibels, so every sample needs a log going in and an exp coming back out.

A common shortcut is to compute the gain only every 16 or 32 samples and slide between values. That cuts the log/exp cost by about 20×, and nobody can hear the difference.

Delay and chorus: the delay line

A delay writes each sample into a ring buffer and reads back the one from D samples ago (500 ms is 24,000 samples). The repeats come from feeding some of that output back into the input, usually through a gentle low-pass so each echo gets darker.

A chorus is the same buffer with a very short delay (about 5–30 ms) whose read point an LFO slides back and forth. The moving read point lands between stored samples, so the code interpolates between neighbours. That's the main extra cost, and the reason chorus sits above plain delay.

The other cost of a delay is memory. A 2-second buffer at 48 kHz is 96,000 floats, about 375 KB. That's nothing on a Pi, but on the Daisy it has to live in the 64 MB of external SDRAM, which is slower to reach than on-chip memory.

Phaser

A phaser runs the signal through a chain of all-pass filters. These leave the volume of every frequency alone but shift their timing by different amounts. Mixed back with the dry signal, the shifted copy cancels some frequencies and leaves notches in the spectrum. An LFO sweeps the filters so the notches move, and that motion is the whoosh.

Tier 3 · a few hundred to a couple of thousand

A decent algorithmic reverb

A real room returns thousands of reflections that blur into a smooth, decaying tail. An algorithmic reverb fakes that with many delay lines of different, unrelated lengths that feed back into each other, so echoes multiply into a dense wash. Each line is as cheap as the delay above. A reverb simply needs a lot of them, plus diffusers and filtering, and good ones add slow modulation so the tail doesn't ring metallically.

Freeverb-style: 8 damped comb filters + 4 all-passes per side. Free and serviceable, though a bit grainy.≈ 100–120

Reverb is also the effect that touches the most memory. A big hall can hold a few hundred KB of delay buffer and visit all of it every sample. The Pi's caches handle that easily. On the Daisy, buffers that spill into SDRAM can make a reverb cost noticeably more cycles than its op count suggests.

Tier 4 · modest, if you use FFTs

Convolution with a body impulse response

Why the piezo needs it. An under-saddle piezo senses vibration right at the saddle, mostly the strings. It misses how the spruce top, the back and the air inside the body shape the sound, and it adds harsh peaks of its own. That's the thin, "quacky" plugged-in tone.

What an impulse response is. An impulse response (IR) captures how a system rings after a single click. The usual way to make a body IR: record the piezo and a good microphone at the same time, then work out the filter that turns one into the other. Running your piezo signal through that filter makes it sound much more like the mic'd guitar. A body IR is typically 20–100 ms long, which is 1,000–5,000 samples at 48 kHz.

Why naive convolution is expensive. Applying an IR means every output sample is a weighted sum of the last N input samples, with the IR as the weights:

y[n] = Σk=0…N−1 h[k] · x[n−k] → N multiplies + N adds per sample

A 50 ms IR is 2,400 taps, so about 4,800 operations per sample. That's half the Daisy's entire budget for one effect.

The FFT trick. Convolution in time is the same as multiplication in frequency. So you collect a block of samples, FFT it, multiply it bin-by-bin with the pre-transformed IR, and inverse-FFT the result. An FFT of size M costs about M·log₂M operations instead of M², so the cost per sample grows with the logarithm of the IR length, not the length itself.

The catch is latency. You can't transform a block until you've collected it, so a 2,048-sample block adds 43 ms of delay, far too much to play through. The fix is partitioned convolution: chop the IR into P short pieces of B samples, transform each once ahead of time, and combine them in the frequency domain. Latency drops to B samples, at the price of P complex multiply-adds per frequency bin. The calculator shows the trade.

Raw arithmetic won't be what limits either box for a pedal like this. On the Daisy the tighter limits are memory speed for long buffers and how much you want to write in C++. On the Pi the real work is getting low, steady latency out of Linux: a real-time audio setup, small buffers, and keeping the web server from stealing time from the audio thread.

How rough are these numbers?

Treating one operation as one cycle is a planning rule, not a benchmark. On the Pi 5, NEON SIMD instructions do four float operations at once, so block-friendly code like FFTs and filter banks can run several times faster than the op count suggests. On both chips, cache misses, branches and function-call overhead push the other way. Audio also runs in blocks (say 64 samples), which spreads the fixed per-block costs thin. Doubling the sample rate to 96 kHz halves the cycles available per sample and roughly doubles the tap count of any IR.
