Guitar FX box · design notes

Where the milliseconds go

Your Pi 5 + Pisound box has two kinds of delay. One is paid on every note you play. The other is paid only when you change the sound. Most of our design decisions come from treating them very differently.

Audio latency
pluck → speaker

Every sample of your playing goes through the box. Presets don't avoid this: the box transforms live sound, it never plays back a finished one. Target: under about 10 ms.

Control latency
tap preset → new sound

Your web app tells the box to switch effects. Nothing waits on this, so 100 ms or even a full second is fine.

01 · The two paths

Audio moves in blocks; control is a slow side channel

The converter turns your signal into samples, and the box gathers them into a buffer (a block of, say, 128 samples). Your effect code runs once per block, then the processed block plays out while the next one fills. That's why the delay is roughly two buffers + about 1 ms of converters.

Pluck a note and watch it go. Then switch presets: the teal packet takes its time, and the audio never stops to wait for it.

02 · How much it matters

A millisecond is about a foot of distance

Sound travels about 1.1 feet per millisecond, so latency feels like standing farther from your amp. Players stand a few feet from amps all the time, so a few milliseconds is invisible. The thresholds below are rough and vary by player.

03 · The acoustic wrinkle

Your guitar is making sound too

With an electric, the amp is all you hear. With an acoustic, the body sound reaches your ears directly, and the amp's slightly late copy adds to it. Adding a delayed copy of a sound to itself cancels some frequencies and boosts others. That's called comb filtering, and it sounds hollow or phasey.

Small delays carve a few wide dips into the tone. Larger delays pack the dips closer together, and past roughly 15 ms your ear stops hearing a tone change and starts hearing a separate echo (slapback). Turning the amp up relative to the guitar makes the dips shallower.

05 · Where clicks come from

Clicks are missed deadlines, not slow math

Each block has to be finished before the previous one runs out, so every buffer is a deadline. Your effect usually needs a small slice of that time. Clicks happen when something else grabs the CPU right as a block should start: the web server, Wi-Fi, disk writes. If the hiccup lasts longer than the slack you have left, the speaker runs dry. You hear that as a click.

Two fixes: a bigger buffer gives more slack per deadline (at the cost of latency), and a real-time kernel lets the audio thread jump ahead of everything else. Try the defaults, then flip one at a time.

06 · What this means for the build

How each idea shaped a decision

Every note pays the audio latency

Pisound HAT on the Pi's pins, no USB interface

Audio goes straight to the Pi over I2S, skipping the USB stack and its extra buffering. Start at 128 samples, about 6 ms from pluck to speaker.

Changing sounds is cheap

A web app that's allowed to be slow

Presets and knob changes travel over WebSockets from your phone. A second to switch sounds is fine, so the UI can be as fancy as you like without touching the audio.

Your guitar is also making sound

Stay under about 10 ms

Keep the amp's copy close behind the guitar body. If the blend sounds hollow at 128, try 64 before blaming the effect.

Clicks come from missed deadlines

Raise the buffer first, tune the kernel second

Going from 64 to 128 costs about 3 ms and buys a lot of slack. Reach for the real-time kernel and CPU pinning only if you still hear clicks.

Heat causes missed deadlines too

Active cooler on the Pi 5

A CPU that throttles mid-song suddenly does less work per block. Keeping it cool keeps the effect load where you measured it.

Expensive work can happen ahead of time

Precompute, then run cheap

Impulse responses, filter designs and AI-written effects are prepared offline. Only the per-sample loop runs live, and a Pi 5 core has plenty of room for it.

Not a latency issue, but don't forget it: the Pisound's input is about 100 kΩ, which is low for a passive piezo pickup and will make it sound thin. If your acoustic has no battery-powered preamp, put a small preamp or DI pedal in front of the box.
