Follow-up to What guitar effects cost to compute

A sample is one number

The biquad filter mixes "the last two input samples and the last two output samples" with five fixed numbers. A natural guess is that each sample is a spectrum, one intensity per frequency, in which case a filter would need a whole vector of per-frequency multipliers, not five scalars. That guess is right about the multipliers and wrong about the sample, and the gap between the two is the central idea of audio filtering.

The short answer

  1. A sample is a single signed number: how far the signal sits above or below zero at one instant. Your guitar's stream is 48,000 of them per second, one after another.
  2. No single sample contains a frequency. Frequency is a pattern across samples: how fast the numbers swing up and down.
  3. The five coefficients are scalars, but they multiply samples from different moments. Delaying a wave by one sample does something different to each frequency: a tiny nudge for a bass note, half a cycle for the highest treble.
  4. So a scalar mix of delayed samples multiplies each frequency by its own number, H(f). That function is your vector of per-frequency modification factors. The filter never stores it; it falls out of the five coefficients. The EQ curve in the compute explainer is a plot of it.

What the number is

Your Ovation's under-saddle piezo produces a voltage that follows the strings' push on the saddle, moment by moment. The onboard preamp boosts it, and the Pisound's analog-to-digital converter measures that voltage 48,000 times a second. Each measurement is one sample. By the time your effect sees it, it's a 32-bit float scaled so the converter's limits are −1 and +1. On the way out, the same kind of number sets roughly where the amp's speaker cone sits at that instant.

The number is signed: positive is a push one way, negative the other, zero is rest. That matters, because two signals can cancel, and every filter below relies on it. It's an amplitude, not an intensity. Loudness is closer to the average of its square over many samples.

Your guitar is mono, so there's one number per instant. A stereo stream has two, one per channel. That's the only sense in which a sample is a vector.

Frequencies add up into one number

A pure 440 Hz tone (the A on the 5th fret of your high E string) is a stream of numbers that swings up and down 440 times a second, so one cycle spans 48,000 ÷ 440 ≈ 109 samples. A 4 kHz tone cycles every 12 samples. When both sound at once, the air pressure is the sum of the two, so the stream is the two streams added sample by sample.

That's why one sample can't tell you its frequencies, the same way one pixel can't tell you what a photo shows. Frequency only exists across a run of samples. The fastest wave 48 kHz sampling can hold is 24 kHz, two samples per cycle: up, down, up, down. That's above human hearing, which is why 48 kHz is enough.

Mixing samples from different moments

Strip the biquad down to the smallest filter there is: average each sample with the one before it.

y[n] = ½·x[n] + ½·x[n−1]

Two weights, both ½, the same for every frequency. Now feed it tones. A slow tone barely changes between neighbouring samples, so the average is almost the sample itself and the tone passes through. A tone near 24 kHz flips sign every sample, so each sample cancels its neighbour and the tone disappears. In between, the cancellation is partial. Two scalar weights made a high-cut (low-pass) filter.

Flip one sign, ½·x[n] − ½·x[n−1], and you get the opposite. Slow tones cancel against their nearly equal neighbour and fast ones survive: a low-cut (high-pass) filter. The weights didn't pick out frequencies. The delay did, because one sample is a different fraction of a cycle for every frequency. The weights only decide what to do with the mismatch.

Why each frequency gets its own multiplier

The two-sample filter treats slow and fast tones differently even though its weights are plain numbers. This section works out exactly how differently, for any frequency and any coefficients, and shows that the answer is always one number per frequency: how much the filter scales that tone. The tool that makes it easy is drawing a tone as an arrow. Complex numbers only appear at the end, as shorthand for those arrows.

Step 1 A filter can't change a tone's frequency

Take one pure tone, x[n] = A·sin(ωn + φ). A is its amplitude, φ is its phase (where in its cycle it starts), and ω is how far through its cycle it moves per sample: ω = 360° × f ÷ 48,000. At 3 kHz that's 22.5° per sample, so one cycle takes 16 samples. (Formulas usually give ω in radians, 2πf ÷ 48,000, but degrees are easier to picture.)

The biquad only ever does three things to its input:

  • Scale: b·x[n] is the same tone with amplitude b·A.
  • Delay: x[n−1] = A·sin(ωn + φ − ω) is the same tone with its phase moved back by ω.
  • Add: two sine waves of the same frequency always add up to one sine wave of that frequency, with some new amplitude and phase.

So whatever the coefficients, a tone at frequency ω comes out as a tone at frequency ω. Only its amplitude and phase can change. Everything a filter does to one frequency is captured by two numbers: a gain (output amplitude ÷ input amplitude) and a phase shift. The remaining steps compute them.

Step 2 Draw a tone as an arrow

With the frequency fixed, a tone is fully described by its amplitude and phase, so draw it as an arrow of length A at angle φ. To get the actual samples, let the arrow spin forward by ω every sample. The height of its tip is the sample value. A sine wave is exactly that: the height of a point going round a circle.

In linear-algebra terms: A·sin(ωn + φ) = (A·cos φ)·sin(ωn) + (A·sin φ)·cos(ωn). Every tone at frequency ω is a combination of the two fixed waves sin(ωn) and cos(ωn), so the tones at one frequency form a two-dimensional vector space. The arrow is a tone's coordinates in that space.

Step 3 Scale, delay and add, done with arrows

  • Scaling a tone by b scales its arrow by b. A negative b flips it round.
  • Delaying a tone by one sample gives the arrow as it was one sample ago, which is the current arrow turned back (clockwise) by ω. Two samples of delay turn it back by 2ω. This is the sense in which a delay is a rotation, and the angle depends on frequency: a sliver for a bass note, 22.5° at 3 kHz, a half turn at 24 kHz.
  • Adding tones adds their arrows tip to tail, because adding tones adds their coordinates.

All the arrows spin at the same rate, so the angles between them never change, and neither does the length of their sum. That's step 1 again, in pictures: the output is a steady tone at the same frequency.

Step 4 The gain at any frequency

Now apply this to y[n] = b0·x[n] + b1·x[n−1] + b2·x[n−2]. Give the input an arrow of length 1, pointing right. The output arrow is then:

b0 × (the arrow) + b1 × (the arrow turned back by ω) + b2 × (the arrow turned back by 2ω)

The length of that sum is the gain at this frequency, and its angle is the phase shift. Change the frequency and only the turning angles change; the coefficients stay put. That is the answer to the original question. The filter has one fixed set of weights, but the delays turn their arrows by a different amount at each frequency, so the sum has a different length at each frequency. Sweep ω, plot the length, and you have the frequency response: the "vector of per-frequency multipliers" you were looking for.

Step 5 Complex numbers are shorthand for arrows

You could do all of this with 2-D vectors and rotation matrices. Complex numbers just make it shorter. Write the arrow (x, y) as the number x + j·y, where j² = −1 (engineers write j because i already means current). Then:

  • multiplying by a real number b scales the arrow;
  • multiplying by ejθ = cos θ + j·sin θ turns it by θ. Multiply it out and you get exactly the 2 × 2 rotation matrix applied to (x, y);
  • adding complex numbers adds arrows.

So the recipe from step 4 becomes one line, with e−jω meaning "turned back by ω":

H(ω) = b0 + b1·e−jω + b2·e−2jω

H(ω) is the output arrow for an input arrow of length 1. Its length |H(ω)| is the gain and its angle is the phase shift. Nothing new has happened: it's the arrow sum written as arithmetic, short enough to evaluate at thousands of frequencies to draw an EQ curve. And since the filter is linear, a chord comes out as each of its tones times that tone's own H.

Feedback The biquad's past outputs

The biquad also feeds back its last two outputs. Move those terms to the left-hand side:

y[n] + a1·y[n−1] + a2·y[n−2] = b0·x[n] + b1·x[n−1] + b2·x[n−2]

Once the filter settles, the output is also a tone at frequency ω (step 1), with some unknown arrow Y. The left side is Y plus its own turned-back copies, Y × (1 + a1·e−jω + a2·e−2jω). The right side is the same for the input arrow X. Set them equal and solve for Y:

Y = X × (b0 + b1·e−jω + b2·e−2jω) ÷ (1 + a1·e−jω + a2·e−2jω)

So a biquad's gain is the length of one arrow sum divided by the length of another. The fraction is a ratio of two quadratics, which is where the name biquad comes from. The next figure draws both sums for real filters.

It does kill 1 kHz, but with three weights you can only put the cancellation in one place and live with whatever that does elsewhere: the bass dies too and the treble is boosted 4×. Feedback lets the second sum nearly copy the first everywhere except right at the notch, so the ratio stays at 1 except where you want it. That's what the two feedback coefficients buy: a sharp, local change from five numbers.

The linear-algebra view

Take a stretch of N samples as a vector x ∈ ℝN. Its coordinates are moments in time, not frequencies. A filter with weights b0, b1, b2 is the linear map y = Tx, where T has b0 on the diagonal, b1 just below it and b2 below that: a banded Toeplitz matrix. Every row is the previous one shifted by one step, because the filter does the same thing at every moment.

Left: the three-tap smoother as an 8 × 8 matrix (darkest = b0, then b1, b2; the faded corners are the wrap-around). Right: the same matrix factored. The pink matrices are the Fourier transform and its inverse, whose columns are sinusoids. The middle matrix is diagonal, and its entries, shaded by size, are the smoother's gain at 8 frequencies: full at 0 Hz, zero at 24 kHz (the middle entry), and the second half mirrors the first because the DFT also lists negative frequencies.

Wrap the band around at the edges and T is circulant. The discrete Fourier transform diagonalizes every circulant matrix: T = F−1 · diag(Ĥ) · F. The sinusoids are the eigenvectors and the eigenvalues Ĥ are the frequency response at N frequencies. Your mental model is exactly diag(Ĥ): the filter written in its eigenbasis, where it is elementwise multiplication. The biquad is the same operator written in the time basis, where it's three diagonals and needs only the last few samples.

Sinusoids diagonalize every such filter at once because they're the eigenvectors of the one-sample shift, and a filter that behaves the same at every moment commutes with shifting. For the endless, non-wrapping stream the same statement holds with the discrete-time Fourier transform in place of the DFT.

Feedback makes it y = A−1Bx with A and B both banded. The inverse of a banded lower-triangular Toeplitz matrix is lower-triangular and dense: every output depends on every past input, with weights that decay. That's the "infinite impulse response" in the name IIR filter. Two feedback coefficients buy the effect of a very long weight vector for two multiplies.

Your vector, for real: the spectrogram

The per-frequency picture does exist; it just has to be computed. Take a window of N samples, taper its edges, run an FFT, and you get N/2 + 1 numbers, one per frequency bin. Slide the window along and stack the results, and you have a spectrogram. Each column is the vector you imagined.

Change the window and watch the trade. To tell 82 Hz from 87 Hz (low E from the F above it) the bins must be under 5 Hz apart, which takes at least 48,000 ÷ 5 ≈ 10,000 samples, about 0.2 s of audio. A short window catches the strum instantly but blurs neighbouring harmonics together; a long one separates every harmonic but smears the attack. This isn't a software limit. It's the time–frequency uncertainty principle, and it means a column always describes a stretch of time, never an instant.

That's why the box filters in the time domain. A biquad answers on every sample using four remembered numbers. A spectral method has to collect a window first, and that wait is latency you'd hear against the guitar's own acoustic sound. The effects that do work on spectra, like FFT convolution for the body IR, pay for it in latency or with the partitioning trick in the compute explainer.

How this shows up in the box

Turning an EQ knob changes H(f) by recomputing five coefficients, off the audio path. The per-sample loop doesn't change. That's the "precompute the expensive parts" rule in action.

A frequency-response plot in the web app can be drawn straight from the coefficients with the formula above. It doesn't need any audio.

Linear filters in a row multiply their H's, so the order of EQ bands doesn't change the result. Compressors, distortion and anything else whose behaviour depends on level aren't linear, so none of this page applies to them directly.
