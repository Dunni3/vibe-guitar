Follow-up to A sample is one number

Four views of one filter

The coefficients of the linear combination decide which frequencies come out louder or quieter, and there's an exact mathematical model of that relationship. This page builds it up from the per-sample loop, and checks each claim by computing the same thing two or three independent ways.

Four complete descriptions of the same filter. Each arrow is a calculation that turns one into another; the sections below go through them in order. The whole subject is called the theory of linear time-invariant (LTI) systems.

§1 · The per-sample loop

The filter only looks backward

Picture the filter as an operator that steps from one moment to the next, with one adjustment: it only uses the past. A live filter receives one new input sample, produces one output sample, and moves on. It can't read x[n+1], because the guitar hasn't played it yet. Offline, on a recording, you can use future samples; live, waiting for them would be latency.

One step of the loop. The output y[n] is computed from the new input, the two previous inputs (dark arrows) and the two previous outputs (pink arrows, the feedback). Then every column slides one place left: this step's y[n] becomes the next step's y[n−1]. The dashed cells haven't happened yet.

In code it's five multiplies and four remembered numbers:

# runs once per sample, 48,000 times a second
y  = b0*x + b1*x1 + b2*x2 - a1*y1 - a2*y2
x2, x1 = x1, x      # remember the last two inputs
y2, y1 = y1, y      # remember the last two outputs
output(y)

So the filter is a small state machine: (new input, 4 remembered numbers) → (output, 4 updated numbers). The recursion feels circular because outputs feed back in, but every output depends only on things that already happened. The next section shows that you can always write it without recursion at all.

§2 · Coefficients → impulse response

Unrolling the recursion

Strip the biquad down to a single feedback term, y[n] = x[n] + a·y[n−1]. The same rule says what y[n−1] is, so substitute it in, then do the same for y[n−2], and keep going:

y[n] = x[n] + a·(x[n−1] + a·y[n−2]) = x[n] + a·x[n−1] + a²·x[n−2] + a³·x[n−3] + …

The recursion is an infinitely long weighted sum of past inputs, with weights 1, a, a², a³, …. As long as |a| < 1 the weights shrink, so inputs from long ago stop mattering. This weight sequence is the impulse response, h[k]. It's what comes out when the input is a single click, (1, 0, 0, 0, …), because then every term of the sum is zero except one.

The input is one click at sample 0 (grey). Each output (blue) is the previous output times a, so the output row is h[k] = ak: the weights of the written-out sum. With a positive the echo keeps its sign and fades; with a negative it flips sign every sample as it fades.

Every linear filter, recursive or not, is completely described by its impulse response, because any input is a sum of scaled, shifted clicks:

y[n] = Σk h[k] · x[n−k] (this is called convolution)

Feedback is a cheap way to get a long h. At a = 0.9, one multiply per sample gives an echo that's still above 1% of its first value 44 samples later. Writing that out without feedback would take 44 or more multiplies.

The same filter, fed a steady tone

A click shows how long the filter remembers. To see what it does to bass and treble, feed the same filter a steady tone instead and watch how big the output gets. A linear filter can't change a tone's frequency, only make it bigger or smaller and shift it slightly in time. So after a short start-up, a tone of one frequency comes out as the same tone at a new size. That ratio of sizes is the filter's gain at that frequency:

gain = output size ÷ input size (size = amplitude, the peak height of the wave)

×1 means unchanged, ×2 twice as big, ×0.5 half as big. Audio plots usually give gain in decibels, dB = 20 × log₁₀(gain), a log scale that matches how loudness is heard:

The two buttons give answers you can check by hand. 0 Hz never changes: feed in 1, 1, 1, … and the output settles at a value y that satisfies the loop's rule, y = 1 + a·y, so y = 1 ÷ (1 − a). 24 kHz is the fastest wave a 48 kHz stream can hold: +1, −1, +1, …. The output settles to +Y, −Y, +Y, …, and the rule gives Y = 1 − a·Y, so Y = 1 ÷ (1 + a). With a positive, each output adds a same-sign share of the last one, which builds up slow signals: a bass boost. With a negative, it builds up signals that flip every sample: a treble boost.

§3 · Impulse response → frequency response

A click's echo holds the whole frequency curve

A click contains every frequency in equal amounts, so its echo is the filter's response to all frequencies at once. The Fourier transform separates them again. For each frequency ω it adds up the echo's samples, each turned back by its delay (the arrows from the last page):

H(ω) = Σk h[k] · e−jωk

For the one-pole filter, h[k] = ak, so this is a geometric series, 1 + a·e−jω + (a·e−jω)² + …, which sums to:

H(ω) = 1 ÷ (1 − a·e−jω)

At 0 Hz, e−jω = 1, giving 1 ÷ (1 − a). At 24 kHz it's −1, giving 1 ÷ (1 + a). Those are the two button results in the steady-tone figure. Doing the same sum for the full biquad gives the ratio from the last page:

H(ω) = (b0 + b1·e−jω + b2·e−2jω) ÷ (1 + a1·e−jω + a2·e−2jω)

This is the model you were after. Split any input into frequencies, X(ω). The filter multiplies each one by its own H(ω), so the output's frequency content is Y(ω) = H(ω) · X(ω). A weighted sum over time (convolution) is the same thing as a multiplication over frequency. The coefficients set H, and H sets the output's frequency composition.

§4 · Coefficients → poles and zeros → frequency response

Poles and zeros: why the curve has its shape

The formula gives the curve. Poles and zeros explain it, and they're how filters are designed. Write z = ejω. The top of H is a quadratic, so it has two roots, called zeros. The bottom has two roots, called poles. With real coefficients, each pair is either two real numbers or a mirror-image pair above and below the horizontal axis. Factored:

H = (z − zero₁)(z − zero₂) ÷ ((z − pole₁)(z − pole₂)) with z = ejω

Now ejω is a point on the unit circle at angle ω: 0 Hz on the right, 12 kHz at the top, 24 kHz on the left. Each factor's size is the distance from that point to a zero or a pole. So, for this form of the filter:

gain at ω = (distance to zero₁ × distance to zero₂) ÷ (distance to pole₁ × distance to pole₂)

Walk the point around the circle. Passing near a zero, the top gets small, so that frequency is cut; a zero right on the circle silences it. Passing near a pole, the bottom gets small, so that frequency is boosted. EQ formulas are recipes for placing these two pairs.

A pole is on or outside the circle, so each echo is at least as big as the last and the output never dies away. The filter is unstable; the frequency curve below no longer describes anything you'd hear.

The gain at every probe position, in dB

The presets sit at 4 kHz, 30° round the circle, so they're easy to see. Angle is proportional to frequency, so everything in a guitar's main range (below about 5 kHz) crowds into the right-hand third of the upper half. Zeros are circles, poles are crosses; each has a mirror image below the axis. Blue lines run from the probe point to the zeros, pink dashed lines to the poles. The gain is the product of the blue lengths divided by the product of the pink ones. Right: the impulse response of the same filter. Drag a pole toward the circle and the peak sharpens while the echo rings longer; push it past the circle and the echo grows.

The pole's distance from the centre is also the echo's decay rate: a pole at radius r makes the impulse response shrink by a factor of r every sample. The one-pole filter in §2 is a single pole at z = a on the horizontal axis, which is why its echo was ak. Closer to the circle means a longer ring and a sharper peak. On or outside the circle, the echo never dies away: the filter is unstable. Every filter in the box needs its poles inside the circle, and the standard EQ formulas guarantee it.

§5 · Why the frequency view is the useful one

Your ear does this analysis too

The inner ear performs a version of this analysis mechanically. The cochlea is a coiled strip that resonates to high frequencies at one end and low frequencies at the other, so what reaches the brain is roughly "how much energy at each frequency, moment to moment". That's why a filter's frequency curve predicts what it will sound like far better than its coefficients do.

For steady sounds the ear is mostly insensitive to phase, the angle of H. That's why EQ plots show only its size. The exception is ringing: a very sharp resonance has a long echo, and you can hear it as a tone that hangs on after each note.
